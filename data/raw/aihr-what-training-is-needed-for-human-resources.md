## What you need to work in HR: The short answer

To work in HR, you need four things: foundational knowledge, a way to prove it, practical skills, and a plan to keep them current. How you get each one depends on where you’re starting. Whichever route you take, your training should cover the same core areas:

- Recruitment and onboarding: Writing job descriptions , interviewing candidates, and bringing new hires up to speed.

- Employment law and compliance: Discrimination and harassment rules, leave, wage and hour law, and workplace safety .

- Employee relations: Handling complaints, conflict, and disciplinary processes fairly and confidentially.

- Compensation and benefits: Pay structures, benefits administration , and payroll basics.

- HR technology and data: HR information systems (HRIS), applicant tracking systems (ATS), spreadsheets, and basic people analytics.

You can learn these areas through a practical certificate program, a certification, a degree, or on the job. What changes is how fast you get there and how easily you can show an employer you can do the work.



## Practical, in-demand HR training

Practical HR training helps you learn to do the work, such as building an onboarding plan, running a structured interview, or turning HR data into a recommendation your manager can act on. It’s also the fastest way to build the skills HR teams say they’re short on.

### HR certifications

An HR certification helps you prove what you know, and you don’t always need experience to qualify. The SHRM-CP and HRCI’s aPHR are both open to people without HR experience. The PHR and SHRM-SCP require professional HR experience, so they usually come later in your career.

The Society for Human Resource Management’s (SHRM) SHRM-CP and SHRM-SCP are behavioral, competency-based credentials recognized across the US. The HR Certification Institute’s (HRCI) PHR and SPHR certifications lean more toward the technical side, testing your grasp of employment law, compliance, and HR operations.

Between them, they cover most of what employers mean when they ask for someone “HR certified.” Our guide to becoming HR certified walks through eligibility for both.

Both are exams. They test what you know, but they aren’t designed to teach you how to apply it at work, so you still need to build that skill somewhere. That’s why it pays to pair a certification with the practical training covered above. Our roundup of the best HR certifications compares options by career stage if you’re not sure where to start.

### Formal education

While a handful of entry-level HR roles will take you on with administrative experience alone, most employers still ask for a bachelor’s degree, and the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) counts human resources, business, and communications among the relevant fields. An HR degree gets you to the fundamentals fastest. A business or psychology degree gets you there too, just by a different route, picking up the commercial thinking or people-reading skills the job also demands.

If you want to test the field before committing to four years, an associate degree or diploma can get you into entry-level roles such as HR assistant or coordinator faster. For management and director-level roles, a master’s degree in HR, organizational leadership, or an MBA becomes more common, mostly because those roles need business fluency an undergraduate program doesn’t always build. Our guide to HR job requirements breaks down what employers expect at each level.

### Do you need a degree to work in HR?

Not always. Many entry-level roles, such as HR Assistant or HR Coordinator, will also consider administrative or customer-facing experience instead of a degree. A degree gets harder to skip as you move toward HR Manager and HR Director roles, so check what the job postings you want actually ask for. Our guide to degrees for Human Resources covers which majors employers favor.

None of this is the whole story, though a degree gives you the theory, and what you do with it next, through applied skill-building and certification, turns that theory into a job you’re actually ready to do.

## Core HR knowledge to develop

Whatever path you take, a degree, an HR certification, or an HR certificate program, you need broad capability across the core competencies every HR role now requires. AIHR’s T-Shaped HR Competency Model , now in its fourth iteration and shaped by AIHR’s global HR community across 140+ countries, outlines six competencies: Business Acumen , Data Literacy , Digital Agility , AI Fluency , People Advocacy , and Execution Excellence .

Business Acumen connects your work to how the organization creates value, Data Literacy turns people data into decisions, Digital Agility and AI Fluency keep you current as HR technology changes, People Advocacy keeps the human side of the job central, and Execution Excellence, the personal effectiveness to follow through consistently, is what turns the other five from knowledge you hold into practice you actually apply.

## Specialist HR knowledge to develop

Core competencies alone aren’t enough. You also need depth on the vertical bar of the T, and that covers two things. The first is specialist expertise in at least one HR domain, whether that’s talent acquisition, compensation and benefits, or HR Generalist work covering a bit of everything.

The second is leadership competencies, such as change leadership and building credibility and trust, which matter once you start managing people.

A Talent Acquisition Specialist without data literacy struggles to turn a hiring plan into a business case a CFO will approve. Someone with strong business acumen and AI fluency but no functional depth can talk strategy convincingly without being able to execute it. Either way, you’re missing half of what the role actually needs.



## The skills gap most HR training misses

Even HR professionals with real depth on the vertical bar often find themselves short on the horizontal one, the core competencies that determine how far that specialty goes. AIHR’s Future-Ready HR Skills Report , based on research with 961 HR teams and 13,665 HR professionals, found that only 50% of HR teams believe they have the right skills to deliver the impact the business expects of them.

Three capabilities consistently fall short: digital agility is the lowest-rated skill across the population, data literacy ranks second-lowest, and business acumen shows uneven growth even among experienced professionals. All three are core competencies in the T-Shaped model, and every strategic HR role depends on them.

Generic upskilling doesn’t hold up against the complexity of real HR work. Off-the-shelf e-learning explains concepts but skips the complexity, and seniority-based development assumes skills grow with tenure , even though the data show they don’t.

## HR training requirements by career stage

What you need to work in HR changes as you move up. Use the stage you’re at now to choose your next step, not the one after it.

### Entry level: HR Assistant or HR Coordinator

Focus on HR fundamentals: onboarding, employee records, HR systems, and basic employment law. A bachelor’s degree helps but isn’t always required at this stage. AIHR’s HR Coordinator Certificate Program covers the day-to-day operations these roles run on, and the aPHR or SHRM-CP can strengthen a thin résumé.

### Mid-career: HR Generalist or HR Specialist

This is where breadth or depth starts to matter. Generalists need a working grasp of every core HR area, while specialists go deep in one, such as talent acquisition or compensation and benefits. The HR Generalist Certificate Program or a domain-specific program builds that range, and the PHR or SHRM-CP can validate it.

### Senior: HR Manager, HRBP, or HR Director

Senior roles shift from running processes to shaping strategy, so business acumen and data literacy carry more weight. Programs like HR Business Partner 2.0 (https://www.aihr.com/courses/hr-business-partner-certificate/) or the HR Manager Certificate Program build the strategic side. A master’s degree or MBA is more common at this level, as is the SHRM-SCP or SPHR.

## Keeping your training current: HR recertification credits

Earning a certification is a milestone, not the finish line. SHRM-CP and SHRM-SCP holders need 60 PDCs every three years , or they must retake the exam. HRCI certifications also run on a three-year cycle. The PHR, for example, requires 60 recertification credits.

That’s a good reason to choose training that does double duty. AIHR is recognized by SHRM to offer PDCs for SHRM-CP or SHRM-SCP recertification activities, and its programs are also eligible for recertification credits with HRCI , HRPA, CPHR, and ATD.
